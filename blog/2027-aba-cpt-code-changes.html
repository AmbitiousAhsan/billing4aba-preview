The 2027 ABA CPT code changes: what to settle with each payer before January 1

The calendar turns on January 1, but claims do not change themselves. An authorization may span the year. The electronic health record may still carry old service names. A payer may load the new codes on one date and issue its provider bulletin on another.

The American Medical Association (AMA) released the 2027 Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) code set on September 9, 2026. The 2027 set applies to dates of service on or after January 1, 2027. For applied behavior analysis (ABA) practices, the work now is payer-by-payer readiness.

This article is not coding advice. Before changing billing, use the official CPT 2027 guidance, the ABA Coding Coalition's supplemental guidance, and each payer's written policy.

What is changing nationally

The ABA Coding Coalition's public summary lists six new adaptive behavior services codes: 97148, 97149, 97159, 97160, 97173, and 97180. It identifies 97149 and 97160 as add-on codes and describes revisions within the existing code family. Treat that page as a Coalition summary, not a substitute for the official CPT source and its complete instructions.

The Coalition's materials summarize changes involving technician direction and analysis, certain face-to-face treatment with analysis, services involving harmful behavior and multiple technicians, and specified non-face-to-face professional work. This is orientation only. Confirm descriptors, parenthetical instructions, time rules, add-on relationships, definitions, and reporting guidance in the official CPT 2027 source before use.

What is not settled nationally

A national code does not create one national payment policy. Each state Medicaid program, commercial plan, TRICARE contractor, contract, and claims system may publish its own coverage, authorization, rate, modifier, and implementation instructions.

Medicare payment material does not establish Medicaid, commercial, or TRICARE policy. Use a current final Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services source only for the Medicare question it actually answers, and use each payer's own written bulletin for that payer and product.

in one sentenceA new code does not guarantee payment or coverage. A payer can recognize a valid code while applying its own medical-necessity, provider, authorization, documentation, and contract terms.

Ask every payer the same questions

Build one row per payer and product. Send questions in writing:

  • On what date will your claims system accept the 2027 adaptive behavior services codes?
  • Which 2026 codes become invalid for dates of service on or after January 1, 2027?
  • What crosswalk will you use for existing authorizations?
  • Will authorizations that span January 1 convert automatically?
  • If not, when and how should replacement requests be submitted?
  • Which new or revised codes are covered for this product?
  • What provider types, modifiers, places of service, and documentation rules apply?
  • What rates apply, and is a contract amendment required?
  • How will corrected or late claims for 2026 dates of service be handled after the transition?
  • Is there a published bulletin, manual revision, or configuration notice you can cite?

Do not coordinate rates with other practices. Review your own contract and negotiate independently.

The code change sits in the Clean claims stage, but the work begins in payer files, authorizations, scheduling, documentation, and systems.

Build an internal change list

Use a controlled sequence:

  1. 1First confirm the official CPT guidance.
  2. 2Next collect the payer's published policy or written answer.
  3. 3Then map the confirmed rule to the applicable electronic health record, practice-management system, clearinghouse, claim edits, authorizations, and staff instructions.

Assign one owner to the evidence trail.

For each payer, track:

  • Source link or written response
  • Publication or response date
  • Product and state scope
  • Effective date
  • Authorization conversion rule
  • Covered code list
  • Contract or rate action
  • Electronic health record change owner
  • Clearinghouse or claim-edit test
  • Staff training date
  • Unresolved question and escalation owner

Keep "unknown" visible. Do not convert silence into an assumption.

Protect authorizations that cross January 1

  1. 1Run a report of authorizations active on January 1, 2027.
  2. 2For each one, compare the approved 2026 code with the payer's written transition rule.
  3. 3Ask whether the payer will map it, replace it, or require a new request.

Use the payer's current authorization form, transition notice, and written policy as the controlling sources.

Check the authorization number, codes, units, dates, provider, location, and service against the January schedule. A technically valid 2027 code can still fail when the authorization remains tied to a retired or revised code.

Update systems without erasing 2026 history

Do not simply rename an old service code. Preserve the code used for 2026 dates of service and create controlled 2027 records according to your system's design and applicable vendor instructions. The Coalition's provider suggestions also emphasize obtaining first-hand payer information before implementation.

Test:

  • Date-of-service logic at December 31 and January 1
  • Code and add-on relationships
  • Units and time calculations
  • Provider and modifier rules
  • Authorization matching
  • Claim-file output
  • Clearinghouse edits
  • Payment posting and denial categories

Use test data, not a live patient claim, when your system allows it. Keep the configuration record and approval source.

The live top 10 CPT codes guide describes the 2026 set. For 2027 dates of service, use current official CPT guidance and the payer's written policy instead.

Where this usually breaks

The transition breaks when one team updates the fee schedule, another keeps the old authorization, and the scheduler does not know which services changed. A payer matrix and a date-controlled system change give everyone one source.

Train clinicians and billers on the exact changes relevant to their work. Avoid asking staff to memorize the entire code set. Give them the payer rule, effective date, documentation change, and escalation path.

in one sentenceJanuary 1 is an effective date, not a readiness strategy. Get each payer's answer, preserve the evidence, and keep uncertain rows on hold.

You do not need every answer in one day. You do need a clear source for each change before it reaches a session, authorization, or claim.
